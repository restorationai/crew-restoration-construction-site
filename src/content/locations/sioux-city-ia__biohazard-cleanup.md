## Why Biohazard Situations Require a Different Approach Here

Sioux City's housing stock runs older in many of its core residential blocks, built in the early-to-mid 1900s with plaster walls, hardwood subfloors, and absorbent baseboards that don't behave like modern finishes. Blood, bodily fluids, and other biohazardous material can wick into these older materials faster than into sealed drywall or laminate, which changes what has to be removed versus what can be cleaned and disinfected. Rental properties and multi-unit buildings near the city's older commercial corridors add another layer: shared HVAC returns and tight unit spacing mean odor and contamination control has to be handled carefully to avoid affecting neighboring units.

## Our Biohazard Cleanup Process

Every job starts with an assessment of what's affected and what local and state regulations require for handling and disposal of biohazardous waste, including blood, sharps, and other infectious material. Technicians use PPE appropriate to the exposure, remove and bag regulated waste for proper transport, and apply EPA-registered disinfectants to surfaces that can be salvaged. Porous materials that cannot be reliably decontaminated, such as saturated carpet pad or damaged drywall, are removed and disposed of according to Iowa's regulated medical waste guidelines. The process closes with a deodorization step and a final inspection before the space is handed back.

## Local note

Woodbury County's regulated medical waste disposal requirements mean sharps, blood-soaked materials, and other infectious waste can't just go into a standard dumpster, they have to be transported by a licensed medical waste hauler to an approved facility. That adds a coordination step that doesn't exist with ordinary debris removal, and it's one reason biohazard jobs take planning that a typical cleanout doesn't.
