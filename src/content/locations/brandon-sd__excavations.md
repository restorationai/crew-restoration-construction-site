## Why Brandon Soil Complicates Excavation

Brandon sits on glacial till, a dense mix of clay and compacted sediment left behind by the last ice age, and it behaves differently than the sandier soil found closer to the Big Sioux River in Sioux Falls. Clay-heavy till holds water, which means excavation for foundation repair, French drains, or utility line replacement often hits saturated subsoil even weeks after the last rain. In the Split Rock area, lots that back up to the creek corridor see a higher water table for much of the spring, and excavation crews need to plan for sump pumping and shoring before they can safely open a trench. South Dakota's frost depth also matters here. Footings and utility lines need to sit below the frost line, and in a fast-growing community like Brandon where many homes in Brandon Valley and Aspen Heights were built in the last decade, that depth was set to current code, but older additions and detached structures sometimes weren't.

## Our Excavation Process in Brandon

We start with a site walk to confirm utility locates, grading, and where water actually moves across the property, not just where the drainage plan says it should. From there we pull permits through Minnehaha County where applicable, stage equipment with enough clearance for Brandon's typically wider suburban lots, and excavate in stages rather than opening the full trench at once, which keeps clay walls from sloughing in wet conditions. Spoil is kept separate from backfill material so we're not reintroducing saturated clay next to a foundation we just repaired. Once the excavation is open, our IICRC-trained team documents moisture conditions at the footing or slab line, which matters if the dig connects to a water intrusion claim rather than a standalone drainage project. Backfill is compacted in lifts, not dumped and graded flat, because a shortcut there is exactly what causes the settling cracks homeowners call us about two years later.

Several of Brandon's newer subdivisions, particularly around Aspen Heights, are governed by HOA covenants that require approval before ground is disturbed for drainage work, utility trenching, or grading changes near shared lot lines. We've learned to request that paperwork early rather than after equipment is already on site, since HOA turnaround can add a week or more to a project timeline if it's not started up front. For work tied to county right-of-way or that affects setback distances, we coordinate permitting through Minnehaha County before mobilizing.

## Local note

Lots along the Split Rock Creek corridor and near Aspen Park tend to have a shallower topsoil layer than the rest of Brandon, sometimes just eight to ten inches before hitting dense till.
