## Why Dell Rapids Properties See Flood Damage

Dell Rapids sits on quartzite ledge that runs close to the surface through much of town, which is part of why the river carved the dramatic dells visitors come to see. That same hard rock means stormwater does not soak into the ground the way it would over sandy or loamy soil elsewhere in Minnehaha County. Runoff sheets across yards and into window wells instead, and low-lying lots near Rocky Run and the river channel take on water fast during spring snowmelt and summer cloudbursts alike. Homes in the Downtown Dell Rapids Historic District add another layer of complexity: many were built before modern foundation drainage codes existed, with stone or early poured foundations that were never designed to shed groundwater pressure. When the Big Sioux rises, those basements take on water through old mortar joints and cove gaps rather than through a single obvious entry point, which makes locating every wet pocket part of the job rather than an afterthought.

## Our Flood Damage Restoration Process in Dell Rapids

We start by pulling standing water with truck-mount extraction, then move to moisture mapping so we know what's actually saturated versus what just looks wet on the surface. Quartzite-adjacent foundations and older plaster walls in historic-district homes hold moisture longer than newer drywall construction, so we set drying equipment and verify progress with meters rather than guessing based on how a wall feels to the touch. Materials are dried in place when structurally sound and per IICRC S500 guidelines; anything porous that's been sitting in floodwater, baseboards, carpet pad, insulation, often has to come out because floodwater carries contaminants that clean water intrusions don't. Once moisture readings hold steady across a few days, we move into reconstruction: drywall, trim, flooring, and anything else the flood took out. Our IICRC-trained team handles the documentation along the way, which matters when a claim is tied to a flood policy rather than a standard homeowner's policy.

## Local note

Basements in the Historic District often have a step-down utility room or root cellar built into the original foundation, a layout that was common before 1940s construction norms standardized basement depth. Those sunken rooms are usually the first space to flood and the last to fully dry, because they sit below the main slab and trap both water and humidity longer than the rest of the basement. We treat those rooms as their own drying zone rather than assuming they'll dry at the same rate as the rest of the lower level, which is a detail that matters a lot once drywall and trim go back in.
