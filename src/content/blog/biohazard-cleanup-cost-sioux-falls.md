A sewage backup or biological spill that spreads across a basement or multiple rooms, and requires tear-out, pushes toward the upper end.

If you're standing in a Sioux Falls basement or bathroom looking at the aftermath of a sewage backup, an injury, or an animal-waste situation and wondering what a professional cleanup crew is going to charge, the honest answer is a range, not a flat number. What moves the price for your specific job is mostly three things: what the contamination actually is, how much square footage it touched, and how much material has to be removed versus just cleaned and disinfected in place.

Most Sioux Falls biohazard cleanups fall between $1,500 and $10,000, with the midpoint for a typical single-area job landing around $3,000 to $5,000. The table below breaks out typical scenarios.

These are typical industry ranges for the Sioux Falls and southeastern South Dakota market, not a quote. Every job gets a written scope before work starts, because the real cost depends on what's actually behind the walls and under the flooring once containment goes up.

## What Factors Drive the Cost of Biohazard Cleanup?

Four things move the number more than anything else: what the contaminant is, how far it spread, what materials absorbed it, and how fast the job started.

**Contamination type.** A sewage backup is classified as Category 3, or "black water," under the [ANSI/IICRC S500 standard](https://iicrc.org/s500/), meaning it's treated as grossly contaminated and everything it touched gets evaluated for removal rather than just drying. Blood, bodily fluid, or animal waste cleanups follow bloodborne-pathogen handling protocols regardless of square footage, which affects labor time and disposal cost more than it affects materials.

**Square footage and spread.** A contained spill in one bathroom costs far less than a sewage backup that traveled across a finished basement in an older McKennan Park or Cathedral Historic District home with a single low drain point.

**Materials affected.** Non-porous surfaces like tile, sealed concrete, or metal can often be cleaned and disinfected in place. Porous materials, carpet, carpet pad, drywall, insulation, and subflooring, usually have to be cut out and discarded, because they can't be fully decontaminated. Tear-out and disposal are where the biggest cost swings happen.

**Response time.** The longer contamination sits, the more it spreads into porous material and the more has to come out. A same-day response on a sewage backup or spill almost always costs less than one left for a few days.

## Does Homeowners Insurance Cover Biohazard Cleanup?

It depends on the cause, not the cleanup itself. Most homeowners policies cover biohazard cleanup when it results from a sudden, accidental event, like a sewer backup or an accidental injury, but many policies specifically exclude sewer and drain backups unless you've added a backup endorsement.

According to the [Insurance Information Institute](https://www.iii.org/article/what-is-covered-by-standard-homeowners-insurance), standard homeowners coverage is built around sudden and accidental damage, which is why documentation matters: photos of the affected area before cleanup starts, a written scope of work, and an itemized invoice that separates labor, disposal, and materials. That paperwork is what an adjuster needs to process the claim, and it's worth asking your cleanup company to provide it as a standard part of the job rather than an afterthought.

## What Happens During a Professional Biohazard Cleanup?

A biohazard cleanup follows a set sequence, regardless of the specific contaminant, because the goal is the same: remove the hazard, decontaminate the space, and confirm it's safe to reoccupy.

1. **Assessment and containment.** The crew evaluates the extent of contamination and seals off the work area with poly sheeting to stop cross-contamination into clean parts of the house.
2. **PPE and site prep.** Technicians suit up in Tyvek, respirators, and double gloves per [OSHA's bloodborne pathogen standard](https://www.osha.gov/bloodborne-pathogens), and staging areas go in for clean versus contaminated equipment.
3. **Removal of contaminated materials.** Porous materials that can't be fully disinfected, carpet, pad, affected drywall, come out and go into sealed, labeled biohazard waste containers for proper disposal.
4. **Cleaning and disinfection.** Remaining surfaces are cleaned with EPA-registered disinfectants appropriate for the contaminant, often applied with electrostatic sprayers for even coverage in hard-to-reach areas.
5. **Deodorization and drying.** Air scrubbers and, when needed, dehumidifiers run to clear odor and residual moisture, especially after a sewage event.
6. **Final check.** The crew does a visual and, when warranted, a verification pass before clearing the space for normal use.
